\section{Introduction}

% =============================================================================
% P1: THE GAP
% Argument: Fast benchmarks isolate single concerns. The interesting research
% happens where concerns interact. No fast benchmark tests this.
% =============================================================================

GPU-accelerated reinforcement learning environments have dramatically reduced the cost of running artificial intelligence experiments and training runs.
However, most environments test a narrow band of intelligence, such as continuous control tasks, board games, grid navigation or combinatorial puzzles.
% [CITE: Brax -- continuous locomotion control. Check Freeman et al. 2021 or latest version]
% [CITE: Jumanji -- combinatorial optimization. Check Bonnet et al. 2023]
% [CITE: PGX -- board games. Check Koyamada et al. 2023]
% [CITE: MiniGrid/BabyAI -- grid navigation + language. Check Chevalier-Boisvert et al.]
Most of these operate on short time scales where actions have no lasting consequences.
% [CITE: Craftax -- Matthews et al. 2024]
Craftax is currently the closest thing to a long term GPU accelerated benchmark, testing exploration, survival, and adaptation in a procedurally generated dungeon.
Its scaling complexity ensures it stays useful as methods improve.
However, the skills it tests are fundamentally reactive: navigate terrain, gather supplies, respond to threats.

The most interesting problems in the real world are not like this.
In supply chain management, an allocation decision is simultaneously a resource commitment, a logistics constraint, and a coordination signal to other actors in the network.
% [CITE: look for RL applied to supply chain optimization]
In multi-robot construction, placing a beam is at once a building action, a constraint on future placements, and a dependency for every structure built on top of it.
% [CITE: look for multi-robot construction/assembly RL work. Check Werfel et al. or recent warehouse robotics papers]
What these problems share is that agents consume finite resources to build persistent infrastructure, multiple actors draw from the same shared pools, and the physical structure of what gets built determines what can be maintained and expanded later.
The incentives cannot be decomposed: an agent cannot optimize construction independently of maintenance, or resource allocation independently of coordination with other actors.

Factory-building is a natural setting for exactly these dynamics.
A factory consumes finite raw materials to produce machines that form physical supply chains.
Multiple workers share the same resource deposits and infrastructure.
The spatial layout of the factory determines throughput, and a design that is hard to maintain will degrade and cost future production.
% [CITE: Factorio Learning Environment]
The Factorio Learning Environment demonstrated that these mechanics produce rich, integrated RL challenges, but it wraps the Factorio game engine, limiting both throughput and modifiability.

% =============================================================================
% P2: WHAT FACTORIAX IS
% Argument: Concrete description. Reader should picture the game.
% Factoriax makes the FLE insight fast enough to train on.
% =============================================================================

Factoriax makes this problem structure accessible at GPU scale.
It is a fully JAX-native environment where multiple agents inhabit a shared grid world containing finite deposits of raw materials.
Agents start with nothing. They mine ore by hand, craft it into machines, and place those machines on the map to automate what they were doing manually.
Each new machine opens possibilities but costs materials that are gone permanently, and the physical arrangement of machines on the map determines whether resources flow through the factory or stall at a bottleneck.
As the factory grows, so does its vulnerability: the architecture is designed to support environmental threats that can damage infrastructure, and depleted resource patches force agents to adapt or relocate parts of their production chain.
Machines can produce science packs, a dedicated resource that is the environment's measure of productivity.
Agents can also spend science packs to unlock new technologies in a research tree, gaining access to more advanced machines and recipes.
The benchmark is never solved: the optimization target is fundamentally open, and there is always a more efficient factory to build.
Progress is measurable through a series of milestones spanning the full arc from first ore mined to final product launched.
% [NOTE: milestone design draws on Craftax and MineRL -- cite both if crediting the pattern]
The entire step function is JIT-compiled and vectorized, running parallel rollouts across dozens of environments on GPU.

% =============================================================================
% P3: WHY BUILDING A FACTORY IS HARD
% Argument: The coupled feedback loop (single-agent), then multi-agent
% as an equal concern that multiplies the difficulty.
% =============================================================================

In Factoriax the agent designs the system it will later operate.
Each machine must be placed in the right location, oriented correctly, and connected to its neighbors.
A single misplacement can silently break an entire production chain.
Building that design costs resources that do not come back.
Every construction decision is simultaneously an allocation decision, and the consequences surface only hundreds of steps later when materials run out.
% [TODO: Can you quantify the credit assignment horizon from training runs? e.g., "the earliest resource decision that affects rocket viability occurs on average at step X" -- strong empirical claim if available]
Once running, the factory must be maintained: resources deplete, bottlenecks emerge, and production degrades in ways that require the agent to diagnose \emph{which} part of the chain it built has broken and why.
Design feeds construction, construction constrains maintenance, and maintenance reshapes future design. The loop never closes.

When multiple agents share the same map, these feedback loops interact across agents.
Every resource one agent spends is unavailable to the others. Every machine one agent places occupies space and consumes materials that could have served a different part of the factory.
Agents must divide labor, specialize, and coordinate their construction plans, all while competing for the same finite deposits.
% [CITE: check Melting Pot and Overcooked for multi-agent comparison. Key difference: those have cooperation but no persistent shared infrastructure where one agent's construction physically constrains another's options.]
This is a richer coordination problem than existing multi-agent benchmarks offer: agents are not merely sharing a space, they are co-constructing a system whose performance depends on how well their individual contributions fit together.
A belt network laid by one agent becomes the backbone that another agent's production depends on. A resource patch claimed by one agent forces others to find alternative supply routes.
The division of labor must adapt continuously as the shared factory grows and the map changes beneath it.

% =============================================================================
% P4: RESEARCH OPPORTUNITIES
% Argument: Beyond RL benchmarking, this environment opens doors for
% other research communities. Light touch, 2-4 sentences.
% =============================================================================

Beyond reinforcement learning, the structure of Factoriax lends itself to questions that other benchmarks cannot easily pose.
The environment is playable by humans, creating a natural setting for human-AI collaboration research where people and agents build together on the same map.
% [CITE: look for human-AI teaming / human-in-the-loop RL benchmarks to position against]
Its multi-agent dynamics and configurable incentive structures make it a candidate testbed for alignment and safety research, where observable agent behavior in a complex but interpretable world can be studied under controlled conditions.
% [CITE: look for AI safety benchmarks / multi-agent safety testbeds. Check MARL safety literature.]
Because all game state is represented as structured arrays, agent decision-making is amenable to interpretability techniques that require access to the full causal chain from observation to action to outcome.
% [NOTE: these are directions this paper does not pursue in depth, but they motivate the design choices and signal relevance to a broader audience]

% =============================================================================
% P5: CONTRIBUTIONS
% =============================================================================

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An open-source, fully JAX-native factory-building environment supporting 1 to N agents on a shared map with partial observability, configurable incentives, and open-ended scaling.
    \item A benchmark progression of milestones spanning the full game, providing measurable progress signal while keeping the overall challenge unsolved.
    \item Baseline reinforcement learning results demonstrating that the environment is learnable but far from solved, with analysis of where current methods break down.
    % [TODO: contribution 3 requires running PPO baselines and analyzing failure modes before this is ready]
\end{enumerate}
